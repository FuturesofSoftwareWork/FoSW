# Sector run — ethics, responsibility and society

**Before anything else, read `docs/sector-prompts/sector-prompt-instructions.md`
in full.** It carries the role, the input files, the deduplication rules, the
output contract and schema, the retrieval-report format and the writing
guidance — all of which apply to this run unchanged. This file carries only what
is specific to this sector, and adds to those instructions rather than
overriding them.

Dimension id: **`ethics-responsibility-and-society`**

---

## Your beat

How AI-changed software work is being **decided, contested and answered for**
beyond the choices of one engineering team: who gets a say, who is protected, who
is responsible when things go wrong, who gains or loses access to the profession,
and what the state, the courts and organised workers are doing about it.

## Mission

Surface EARLY, HIGH-SIGNAL developments in the rules, rights and responsibilities
around AI in software work, especially in places a technology-news search does
not look: union contracts, works-council agreements, court and arbitration
rulings, labour ministries, professional bodies and open-source governance.

One ratified clause, or one ruling, that changes what an employer may do to
software workers is worth more than ten essays on AI ethics.

**This project studies how work changes.** Ethics, regulation and society are not
subjects in themselves here. They qualify when they reach the people doing
software work.

---

## Priority track — who gets a say over AI at work

This is the first thing to work on in every run. It follows a published signal,
the Blizzard contracts of September 2026 (`2026-09-14-06`), in which workers won a
right to bargain over AI use. On its own that is one contract at one employer. The
question is whether it is the start of something, and the radar needs independent
cases to tell.

Look for the following, in this order of value:

1. **A clause or ruling being used.** A rollout that was changed, delayed,
   withdrawn or bargained over because of a contract, a works council or a court.
   Nothing like this has been found yet for software workers, so one credible case
   would be the most valuable item this run could produce. Report a failure just as
   readily: a clause invoked with no effect is evidence too.
2. **A new binding right covering software workers at an employer other than
   Microsoft.** The cases known so far are all Microsoft and the Communications
   Workers of America (CWA), and they count as one context, not several.
3. **Limits on dismissal or demotion justified by AI**, from courts, arbitrators
   or labour authorities, especially where the worker does software work.
4. **Early organising with AI as a stated reason**, such as union drives,
   recognition votes or demands. Lower value, because no right exists yet.

**Know which rung each item is on, and make it plain in ordinary words.** The rung
names are for your own reasoning and the retrieval report. They must never appear in
a draft: see *Writing for the public* below.

| Rung | Means | Example wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demanded | A union or group is asking for it | "calls for" |
| Organising | A vote or recognition process, with no contract | "voted to join" |
| Tentative | Agreed, not yet ratified or still under appeal | "tentatively agreed" |
| Binding | Ratified, enacted, or ruled and final | "ratified", "upheld on appeal" |
| Used | Someone invoked it and something happened, or did not | "the union bargained over" |

A demand reported as if it were a right is the error this sector is most prone to.

**Keep contexts apart.** Draft each employer and union as its own item, and name
any shared employer or union in `risksAndCaveats`, so a later reader can see when
three items are really one context.

## Jurisdictions — search all three on every run

Work through each of these deliberately and report what you searched under each,
including the ones that returned nothing. An empty jurisdiction is a valid result.
Do not pad one to balance the others.

### United States

Union contracts and organising among software and technical workers; National
Labor Relations Board (NLRB) decisions; state law on AI and employment; lawsuits
over AI-justified layoffs or over who answers for agent-written code.

Start with: CWA and its CODE-CWA campaign, UPTE (University of California technical
workers), Alphabet Workers Union, AFL-CIO technology work.

### Europe

Europe matters differently from the US, and a US framing can mislead. Where works
councils or consultation duties already apply, the question may be how existing
rights are being used rather than whether a new right was won. Look for named
agreements at software and IT-services employers, and for rulings that suspend or
condition a rollout.

Start with: UNI Europa, ETUC and ETUI; IG Metall and ver.di in Germany; CSE
agreements and rulings in France; CWU, Unite and UTAW in the UK; Unionen and
Sveriges Ingenjörer in Sweden; PROSA in Denmark; Pro, TEK and Insinööriliitto in
Finland. **Finland is of particular interest to this project's readers**, so check
it explicitly, including how the Co-operation Act (yhteistoimintalaki) is being
applied to AI changes.

Search in the local language, because this material is rarely written in English:

- German: `Betriebsvereinbarung KI Softwareentwicklung`, `Betriebsrat Copilot Mitbestimmung`
- French: `accord CSE intelligence artificielle informatique`, `tribunal suspension outil IA CSE`
- Swedish: `kollektivavtal AI IT-branschen`, `Unionen AI förhandling`
- Finnish: `tekoäly yt-neuvottelut ohjelmistoala`, `työehtosopimus tekoäly IT-ala`

### China

Two different things are happening, and they must not be merged:

- **Courts.** Chinese courts have ruled against dismissing or demoting workers on
  the ground that AI can do their job. This is the nearest thing to a limit on
  AI-driven job loss, and it is a limit on the employer's stated reason, not on
  using AI.
- **State employment policy.** The labour ministry and State Council have announced
  measures on AI and employment. As far as reported so far, these are about
  retraining, new occupations and job support, not limits on automation. Report what
  a document actually requires. Never describe support measures as a restriction.

Also look for: what is happening to programmers specifically, including the
pressure on engineers over 35; labour arbitration in the technology sector; and
whether a dedicated policy document on AI's employment impact has been published.

Search in Chinese, because the primary material is there:

- `人工智能 替代 解除劳动合同 判决`
- `AI替代 违法解除 程序员`
- `人工智能 劳动争议 典型案例`
- `人社部 人工智能 就业 文件`
- `程序员 AI 裁员 劳动仲裁`

Prefer primary and independent sources: court publications, the Ministry of Human
Resources and Social Security, the State Council Information Office, Caixin, Sixth
Tone, South China Morning Post. Chinese worker forums such as Maimai, V2EX and Zhihu
carry firsthand accounts, but you may not be able to reach them. Say so if you
cannot.

### Elsewhere

Worth a pass when something surfaces, with no obligation to cover it: India's
IT-employee unions and labour ministry complaints, given how much software work
sits there; Korea; Japan.

## Sector scope — what belongs here

In scope, when the source connects it to software work:

- **Worker voice** — bargaining, works councils, consultation and refusal rights
  over AI (the priority track).
- **Employment protection** — rulings and rules on dismissal, demotion or pay cuts
  justified by AI.
- **State response** — laws and policies that change someone's duties or rights in
  software work. Include deregulation and delay, not only new rules.
- **Responsibility** — who answers when agent-written code causes harm, what
  contracts and insurers say, how liability is settled, and what professional bodies
  now expect.
- **Fairness and access** — who gains or loses from these changes: gender, age,
  disability, geography, and the people unable to enter the profession.
- **Public-interest and open-source work** — how maintainers and foundations are
  sustained or burdened, and the rules they set on AI contributions.
- **Hidden labour** — engineers paid to train the models, and contract work
  rearranged around AI.
- **Environmental cost**, only where it changes a decision in software work, such as
  a team budgeting for energy.

**General layoff news adds nothing, here or anywhere on this site,** and there is far too
much of it. Reject layoff announcements, head counts, trackers and reports that simply
attribute job cuts to AI, however large the numbers, as `out-of-sector`.

A layoff item can qualify in two ways:

- **It explains the mechanism:** how AI was actually used, which tasks or workflows it
  took over, how that led to these roles being cut, and what happened to the remaining
  work. An employer naming AI as its reason is not a mechanism, and neither is cutting
  staff to pay for AI investment. A named person's detailed account counts. Write the
  item about the mechanism, and say whose account it is, since it shows what was said and
  done, and does not prove that AI caused the cuts.
- **It shows a change in rights or duties that concerns dismissal:** a ruling, a contract
  term, a law, or a dispute that tests one. The item must be about that change, with any
  figures for cuts in the background.

Statutory redundancy consultation that an employer simply went through meets neither test
on its own. It qualifies only if the source shows the mechanism, or shows workers or their
representatives contesting AI or changing the outcome, and an account from the workers'
side is much stronger evidence of that than the employer's own.

Out of scope — route to a neighbouring sector, do not surface here:

| If the core claim is about… | It belongs to |
| --- | --- |
| Hiring, job openings, wages as market facts | `careers-occupations-and-labour-markets` |
| What to learn, curricula, training programmes | `skills-knowledge-and-learning` |
| How a team reorganises, what a manager does, internal review rules | `organisation-and-leadership` |
| Cost, ROI, productivity as a business result | `economics-productivity-and-value` |
| How the work feels to the person doing it | `worker-experience-identity-and-wellbeing` |
| Who does which task, people or machines | `nature-and-division-of-work` |

The boundary test: **a labour-market item says what happened to jobs; an item here
says who was entitled to decide, or who has to answer for it.** "A firm cut 160
programmers" adds nothing. "A court ruled the firm could not cite AI as its reason" is
this sector. Many items touch both, so file by what the evidence actually shows.

## Altitude — this sector's failure modes are abstraction and overstatement

**The test: can you name who gained or lost a right, a duty or a protection, say
how binding it is, and show that it reaches people doing software work?**

You need all three.

- **Right altitude:** a named contract, ruling or law, with its status, its text
  where available, and the software workers it covers; a named maintainer's account
  of what their project's AI rule changed; a survey with a real sample showing who
  is adopting and who is left out.
- **Wrong altitude:** AI ethics principles and frameworks; responsible-AI
  statements; think-pieces on the future of work; AI safety and alignment research;
  general AI regulation with no visible effect on anyone's work; consumer harms such
  as deepfakes or chatbot safety.

**The software connection.** Decide which of these applies and record it in the
retrieval report. In the draft, say the same thing in ordinary words, such as "the unit
includes application programmers" or "applying this to software work is our
interpretation", and for the weaker two put it in `risksAndCaveats` as well:

- **Direct** — the source names developers, engineers, QA, IT staff or similar.
- **Mixed** — software workers are part of a wider unit, as at Blizzard.
- **General rule** — a law or ruling that plainly applies to software workers without
  naming them. Say that is your reading. If a case concerned a different occupation,
  such as a graphic designer or a journalist, reject it as `out-of-sector`, marking it
  `reviewable: true` when the legal reasoning would clearly carry over.

Clauses and rulings from other sectors, such as Hollywood writers, news organisations
or metalworkers, do not count, however influential they are. They are not evidence
about software work.

**Tool news is excluded**, as in the generic run. A product's governance feature is
not a signal. A security incident or attack is not a signal unless the source shows a
changed duty or a changed rule for the people doing the work.

## Accuracy — report what the document says

The earlier version of the Blizzard signal repeated three contract terms and an
"industry first" that its sources did not support, because press coverage had
embroidered a union press release. That is the normal way this sector goes wrong, so:

1. **Read the primary document whenever it exists:** the contract, ruling, statute or
   union release. If you could not read it, say so in `risksAndCaveats`.
2. **Attribute contract terms.** "CWA says the contract requires…" is accurate.
   "The contract requires…" claims you have read it. Most contract text is not public,
   and you must say so when it is not.
3. **Quote the operative words** in the summary where you have them, and do not
   paraphrase beyond them. "Bargain over" is not "bargain before", and "notify" is not
   "consult".
4. **Check every "first", "historic" and "landmark"** against the source it is
   credited to, and against earlier cases. Do not repeat one you cannot verify. Check
   who is being credited with it too.
5. **Treat every party as an interested party.** Unions, employers, law firms selling
   compliance advice and state media all have reasons to frame a case their way. Name
   the origin in `risksAndCaveats`. Law-firm alerts are often good on what the law says
   and poor on whether anything has happened.
6. **State media needs a second look.** Xinhua and State Council releases are reliable
   for what the state wishes to announce. A ruling published around a public holiday
   may also be a message. Report the ruling, and look for independent reporting on what
   it changed.
7. **Do not assume a ruling transfers.** Say how far it reaches: one case, an
   appellate decision, or guidance for other courts. China's courts do not follow
   precedent the way common-law courts do, so check whether a case was issued as a
   typical or guiding case. One court's reasoning is not "China has banned" anything.
8. **Mark your translations.** If you translated a quotation yourself, say so.

Where sources disagree on a basic fact, such as a worker's job title or a date, say so
rather than picking one.

## Writing for the public

Everything in a draft may reach the live site, so write it for a reader who has never
seen this prompt.

- **No pipeline vocabulary in a draft.** Never write "rung", "Used", "Binding", "software
  connection", "general rule", "priority track", "this search" or "this run". Say what
  happened: "ratified", "filed grievances, with no outcome reported", "applying this to
  software work is our interpretation". Do not address the reviewer. A note such as "a
  reviewer may file this elsewhere" belongs in the retrieval report.
- **Research problems go in the retrieval report.** Failed fetches, walled pages, tool
  limits and undecodable files belong there. A reader needs only what affects trust in
  the item: "we did not read the ruling", "the contract text is not public".
- **Quote in English.** Give foreign-language quotations in English, marked as our
  translation. Put the original wording in the retrieval report so the reviewer can check
  it. An official document number may stay in its original script.
- **Keep titles to one plain claim,** about 25 words at most. Do not end a title with a
  verdict or a label. Qualifiers belong in the summary unless the title would mislead
  without them.

## Signal freshness

Rulings, contracts and policy move slowly, so the window here is the last **45 days**.

**On the first run only, the window for the priority track goes back to 1 January
2026**, because nobody has searched this ground before. You may also draft the original
ZeniMax agreement as a baseline, even though it is older, and it must say in its summary
that it predates the current wave, with `signalStage: "lagging"`.

After the first run, older items qualify only through a new development such as an
appeal, enforcement or a first use.

## Source mix

- **At least half the items must rest on a primary document or a firsthand account:** a
  contract or union document, a ruling, a statute or ministry text, or a named person
  describing what happened to them. Secondary reporting may carry the rest, when it is
  independent journalism with named subjects.
- **At least half the items should come from the priority track**, if that many clear
  the bar. Do not lower the bar to reach it.
- **Academic work:** up to 2 items, judged by when the data was collected.
- **No aggregators as a source.** This topic is heavily recycled by AI-written recap
  sites. Use them to find the original, then cite the original.
- **Commercial-intent discount.** Governance vendors and compliance consultancies publish
  heavily on this subject, and their surveys tend to find the gap their product fills.
- **Always include a working `sourceUrl`** and verify it resolves. A court or ministry
  page in Chinese is a valid source.

## Filtering

Output an item only if it bears on the rights, duties or responsibilities of people doing
software work, or on who can do that work, AND at least one of:

- a new or changed binding right, duty or protection
- a dispute, ruling or enforcement action
- a clause or rule being used, or failing when used
- organised workers seeking a say over AI, with AI as a stated reason
- measured evidence of who gains or loses from the change
- a firsthand account of a right, duty or responsibility changing in practice

Reject items that are:

- ethics principles, frameworks and position papers with nothing binding behind them
- general AI regulation with no visible effect on software work
- clauses and rulings from other sectors (`out-of-sector`)
- general layoff news that fails the tests above, and hiring news with no dispute, right
  or duty in it (`out-of-sector`)
- proposed model clauses and toolkits with no adopter (`illustrative-not-measured`)
- vendor governance content with no original data (`commercial-intent`)
- recycled recaps of a case you could read directly (`aggregator-used-primary-instead`)

## Hazards

**Hope and alarm both distort this sector.** Labour sources tend to read a clause as a
victory and a job loss as AI's doing, and employer sources do the reverse. Therefore:

1. **Actively hunt disconfirming cases:** a court that upheld an AI-justified dismissal;
   a clause that changed nothing; software workers voting against a union; a rollout
   jointly agreed without conflict; a government moving to weaken a protection. Say in the
   retrieval report what you looked for and what you found.
2. **Do not count a stated reason as a cause.** An employer citing AI, or a worker blaming
   it, shows what was said. Say so.
3. **Do not imply that the unorganised are covered.** Most software workers in many
   countries have no union, so a clause speaks for those it covers. Say what share or how
   many, where the source gives it.

---

## Ranking — prefer in this order

1. A clause, right or ruling being used in practice for software workers, whatever the
   outcome
2. A new binding right or ruling covering software workers in a new context
3. Disconfirming cases of the same kind
4. Measured evidence of who gains or loses from these changes
5. Firsthand accounts of responsibility or duties changing, including maintainers
6. Organising and demands with AI as a stated reason
7. State policy that has yet to change anything, with its text read

Aim for **3–8 items on the first run and 2–5 after that**. Quality over volume. An empty
run is valid, and on this ground it would itself be informative. Report it honestly.

## Sector notes on the shared instructions

- **Signal types.** Use `regulation-standard` for a contract, ruling, statute or policy with a
  real date. `effectiveDate` must be a date the source states. If only the date of
  announcement or judgment is known, use that and say so in `risksAndCaveats`. If an
  announcement has no date or text at all, do not invent one: log it with `reviewable: true`.
  Use `market-event` for organising votes, naming the organisation and how many workers.
  Use `practitioner-account` for a named person's own account.
- **Category.** `Ethics & Policy` will usually be the primary `category`. File honestly.
- **`recommendedActions`** are often `[]` here. Do not advise employers on how to head off
  organising or monitor where workers might organise. Do not give legal advice. "Check what
  your consultation duties already require" is about as far as a signal should go.
- **This run does not propose phenomena.** It produces signals. Whether they support a
  phenomenon is decided later in a separate clustering session, and which radar sector that
  phenomenon sits in is decided there too, by what it claims. Do not shape an item to fill a
  sector or to support a thesis.
- **The retrieval report's source-mix check** means, for this sector: how many items rest on a
  primary document or firsthand account, how many are on the priority track, and what you
  searched under each jurisdiction. Add a section on which rung each priority-track item is on.
